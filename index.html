Keynote Speakers

Keynote 1

Benjamin Fung

School of Information Studies
McGill University

Research website

AI for Cybersecurity and National Security

OXAI 2026 · November 11, 2026 · Toronto, Canada

Abstract

AI is reshaping the domains of cybersecurity and national security. Benjamin's presentation explores three interconnected dimensions of this transformation. The first draws on his current work with Defence Research and Development Canada (DRDC), applying advanced AI methods to software and vulnerability analyses to strengthen the resilience of cyber-physical systems. The second turns to the geopolitical sphere, examining how foreign states have used generative AI in cognitive warfare and what lessons this holds for protecting Canada’s democratic processes from foreign interference. The third is a software demonstration of AI-powered visualization tools for unstructured open-source intelligence. Taken together, these three topics illustrate how AI simultaneously empowers defenders and adversaries.

Biography

Benjamin Fung is a Full Professor in the School of Information Studies (SIS) and an Associate Member in the School of Computer Science (SOCS) at McGill University. He is the Editor-in-Chief of Elsevier Sustainable Cities and Society: Advances (SCSADV) and an Associate Editor of ACM Transactions on Knowledge Discovery from Data (TKDD). He received a Ph.D. degree in computing science from Simon Fraser University in 2007, and was the Canada Research Chair in Data Mining for Cybersecurity from 2015 to 2025. Collaborating closely with the national defense, transportation, and healthcare sectors, he has over 300 refereed publications, with more than 19,000 citations (h-index 66), that span the research areas of data mining, machine learning, privacy protection, cybersecurity, and building engineering. His data mining works in crime investigation and authorship analysis have been reported by the media worldwide. For more information, visit his research website.

Keynote 2

Blessing Ogbuokiri

Department of Computer Science
Brock University

Beyond Demographics: Rethinking Fairness and Trustworthiness in AI - From Representation Geometry to Real-World Decisions

OXAI 2026 · November 11, 2026 · Toronto, Canada

Abstract

Artificial intelligence systems are increasingly deployed across diverse populations, institutions, and domains, yet conventional approaches to algorithmic fairness often focus primarily on predefined demographic groups and downstream prediction outcomes. This talk presents a broader perspective on fairness and trustworthiness in AI, examining how bias can emerge within learned representations, propagate into model predictions and explanations, and change as models move across domains and deployment settings. Drawing on recent research in contrastive and self-supervised learning, foundation models, healthcare AI, and explainable AI, the talk will explore methods for discovering latent subgroup disparities, measuring bias in representation spaces, and mitigating unfairness through fairness-aware sampling and optimization. It will also examine how apparently strong global performance can conceal subgroup disparities, including across healthcare institutions, and how model explanations may become unstable under domain shift even when predictive performance remains comparable. Together, these studies motivate a shift from viewing fairness as a single downstream metric toward treating it as a property that should be examined throughout the AI pipeline, from representation geometry to real-world decisions. The talk concludes by outlining ongoing directions toward AI systems that are not only accurate, but also fair, interpretable, robust, and trustworthy across diverse populations and contexts.

Biography

Dr. Blessing Ogbuokiri is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Computer Science at Brock University, Canada, and Director of the Responsible and Applied Machine Learning Laboratory (RAML Lab). His research expertise lies in responsible artificial intelligence (AI), AI safety, and machine learning, with a focus on fairness, transparency, and the trustworthy deployment of AI systems. He also works on advancing theoretical computing methods to improve the interpretability, fairness, and scalability of AI models. His research bridges fundamental algorithmic advances with real-world applications, particularly in healthcare and social good. Dr. Ogbuokiri has secured over $1.2 million in research funding, including support from the CIFAR AI Safety Solutions Network, the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada (NSERC) Discovery Grants Program, the New Frontiers in Research Fund (NFRF), and Brock University. His research program seeks to advance AI systems that are not only accurate and scalable but also fair, interpretable, and trustworthy across diverse populations and real-world contexts.

OXAI 2026

Call for Contributions

How can we make the behaviour and decisions of AI systems observable, explainable, and accountable?

OXAI 2026 brings together researchers, practitioners, and other stakeholders to advance the state of the art and best practices in observability and explainability of AI systems. Observability provides the measurements, traces, and runtime evidence needed to understand internal states and behaviours. Explainability turns that evidence into understandable accounts of automated decisions, supporting trust, accountability, and compliance.

Building on OXAI 2024, this edition focuses on agentic AI systems that plan, use tools, maintain memory, and adapt under competing objectives. Particular attention will be given to explainable multi-objective optimization and to making trade-offs among quality, latency, cost, safety, privacy, security, sustainability, fairness, and accessibility observable and understandable.

We invite contributions on methods, architectures, tools, evaluations, governance mechanisms, and engineering practices. Our system-level perspective connects models and services with software architecture, cloud computing, data management, testing, operations, and responsible AI.

Topics of interest

Topics include, but are not limited to:

  • Observability in AI and Agent Systems: monitoring, logging, tracing, provenance, security telemetry, incident response, AgentOps/LLMOps/AIOps, and analysis of plans, memory, tool calls, interactions, and side effects.
  • Explainability in AI Engineering: methods for explaining models and system behaviour, human-centred and accessible explanations, causal diagnosis, and metrics for explanation quality.
  • Explainable Multi-Objective Optimization: agent planning, learning, and runtime adaptation under competing objectives; Pareto trade-offs; stakeholder-dependent preferences; and explanations of constraints and decisions.
  • Human-Centred Quality Attributes and Governance: integrating equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI) by design, fairness, and accessibility into stakeholder requirements, data, interfaces, and evaluation, while addressing privacy, regulatory compliance, oversight, recourse, contestability, and societal impacts of opaque AI systems.
  • Case Studies, Tools, and Applications: empirical studies, benchmarks, demonstrations, experience reports, and industrial and cybersecurity applications of observable and explainable AI.

Participation and workshop format

We welcome academic scholars, industry professionals, government researchers, and community-facing practitioners, including data scientists, machine learning engineers, software architects and developers, AIOps/DevOps practitioners, and researchers in responsible AI, cybersecurity, and governance. Participants at all experience levels are welcome.

The workshop combines two keynotes with sessions on understanding and explaining agent behaviour, diagnosing AI systems, and evaluating their outputs. See the program for presentation times and session chairs.

How to Submit

We accept two submission types:

  • Talk abstract: up to 2 pages, outlining a proposed talk or demonstration.
  • Paper: up to 4 pages, presenting research, work in progress, new ideas supported by evidence, experience reports, or industrial case studies.

Both limits include all text, tables, figures, references, appendices, and other content.

Submit a single PDF in IEEE double-column conference format (24pt title, 10pt body text). For LaTeX, use \documentclass[10pt,conference]{IEEEtran} without compsoc or compsocconf. Submissions are not anonymous: include the title and all authors’ names, affiliations, email addresses, and public profile links in both talk abstracts and papers.

Following IEEE guidance on AI-generated content, disclose such content in the acknowledgments, identifying the system used and affected sections, and cite the system where applicable. At least one author of each accepted contribution must register and present at the workshop.
